Joshua Mendoza Scripting, Hooks & Storyboarding Framework

This is a comprehensive SOP guide, breaking down my exact creation process, as well as additional notes for scripting, hooks, storyboarding, shot lists, formatting, and B-roll. Everything below it is reference, not a checklist to follow in order, pull from whichever section a video actually needs.

Before Anything Else

Concept First

Most people start with the camera. That's actually the last thing to think about. It starts with a concept, what do you want to say, what's the message. Then the emotion, what do you want the viewer to take away after the video. Once you know that emotion, you work backward from it to build everything else: the story, the hook, the Pillar, the Packaging, the format.

Concept first. Emotion second. Everything else follows. Every time. No exceptions.

Get the concept wrong and everything downstream, the hook, the shot list, the format, inherits that mistake. Get it right and the rest of this document is just execution.

Notion

Each one starts differently because each one is solving a different problem. Film/Cinematic and 1-1 Walkthrough both move straight from Concept into Packaging and a full Script plus Shot List, because both get fully planned before a camera ever turns on. Raw still goes to Packaging and Bullet Points, you still have to decide where you're actually going to record it, but there's no shot list to review, that's just where it happens. Instead of planning shots, you rehearse the delivery. Hyper-Edited starts with Concept and Audio chosen together, since these usually begin with a sound or a trend first. Then everything else, the script, the Packaging, gets built around that audio choice. That's why Packaging shows up later in its chain instead of right after the concept like the others.

Section 01

Scripting Notes

The Big Idea

I stick to bullet points. A full script makes me sound fake, not like myself. Bullet points let my real personality come through, and let me say what I actually want to say.

This is also why I don't record a big batch of videos at once. Bullet points only work if the delivery feels fresh, and that freshness doesn't survive ten videos back to back. I record only a couple, go through the entire revision process, and pick the best video from there. I don't batch record because it slows down how fast I actually get better. The real improvement happens between each video, not inside one big batch.

There's also a more practical reason. If something's off in the prep work, a wrong ISO, the saturation isn't dialed in, anything in the camera settings, that mistake doesn't ruin one video. It ruins the entire batch, every single one you recorded that day, because they all share the same mistake. Batch ten videos and get one thing wrong, and you've lost ten chances to catch it, not one. Record one at a time and that same mistake only costs you a single video, and you fix it before the next one. Ten tries, one at a time, will always beat one big batch. It's not even close.

The concept, the one takeaway I want the viewer to leave with, decided first, before anything else gets written. Then the bullet points, the script.

A script alone isn't a finished video. It's one of several things that need to be dialed in before filming, this is just the first.

The Rules

Inside the bullets themselves, I run on a repeatable four-beat pattern.

01
Context
Context about the situation, this can double as the On-Screen Hook.
02
Conflict
Who or what is the antagonist?
03
Solution
The new realization or perspective. Overcoming the conflict.
04
Clarity
A valuable new small takeaway.

A short-form video is typically one or two passes through this cycle. A long-form video is the same loop, just over and over and over again, so that it builds toward a bigger, more exciting story.

Fill-In Shape

"[Context] is where I started. Then [conflict] hit, and it nearly stopped me. What actually got me through it was [solution]. If you're in that same spot: [clarity]."

Same cycle, paired with a few of the hook patterns already in this SOP.

Clarity. Virality without value never converts.

Context. I used to write full scripts before every video.
Conflict. Every take felt stiff, like I was reading instead of talking.
Solution. I switched to bullet points and let the delivery stay loose.
Clarity. The second you memorize a script, you lose the human in it.
Going Deeper
Contrast vs. Conflict

Contrast vs. Conflict is a hook and scripting pattern. It creates tension between an idea and a problem, and that tension is what turns a bullet point into a real story. Contrast is the gap between what people commonly believe and what's actually true. It's a mental gap, and it's what makes you memorable. Conflict is an actual problem you had to solve. It's a story gap, and it's what makes you human. The Conflict beat above is built for that second one. But Contrast can show up anywhere in the cycle, often inside Context or Clarity, any time you're setting something up or landing on something that goes against what people already believe. Use both inside the script itself. That back-and-forth is what keeps people watching all the way through, not just during the hook.

Absorption Rate

How much impact can you pack into one word? Fewer words that still land clearly make you sound more powerful as a speaker. Ask yourself, could this line get cut without losing the idea? If yes, cut it. Use active voice only. Clarity creates context, context creates conviction, conviction converts. One line that actually sticks is worth more than three that get forgotten the moment the video ends.

Every video should leave the viewer with two things: something they can use immediately, and a belief that shifts permanently.
Section 02

Hook Notes

The Big Idea

People decide whether they want to watch you before you even open your mouth.

A hook isn't just the first sentence. It's every piece of stimulation in the first three seconds. Three layers, working at once.

Visual
Everything the viewer can see, before a word is spoken.
On-Screen Hook
The text and subtitles that create the open loop.
Audio
What's said, scored, and heard underneath it all.
All Three, At Once
01Visual
Everything the viewer can see, you, your body language, how you look physically, your Packaging, where you actually are, and everything working under the surface that they don't consciously notice. People who are naturally attractive already have a piece of this working in their favor before they say a word.
02On-Screen Hook
The text and subtitles added in post that create the loop. The viewer's eye moves between the on-screen text, the visual, and the audio, and that back-and-forth is what creates the open loop pulling them in. Think of it as a mini text thumbnail sitting on top of the video itself. It buys a few more seconds of attention, or it points the direction of the video and lets curiosity do the rest.
03Audio
What's actually being said, after those first three seconds, whatever open loop, context, or clarity you're creating with your voice. Plus everything you hear underneath that: sound effects, risers, buildup, music. All of it plays a factor.
The Rules
The Alignment Rule

A hook is actually three things happening at the same time, the Visual, the On-Screen Hook, and the Audio, the same three from above. All three have to point in the same direction. If the Visual feels calm but the Audio sounds urgent, or the On-Screen Hook says one thing while you're saying something else out loud, the viewer's brain has to stop and sort it out. That split second of sorting is exactly when they scroll away. Your eyes process the Visual first, faster than you can read or hear anything, so whatever it's communicating sets the tone for the other two. Get all three saying the same thing and the hook feels instant. Get even one out of sync and it feels off, even if the viewer couldn't tell you why.

Stop, Then Context

You have to grab someone's attention in the first couple of seconds, that's Stop. Motion, color, a strong image, whatever physically catches the eye and freezes a scroll before a single word gets read or heard. Right after that comes Context. Your first sentence has to say what this is actually about. Skip Context and nothing that follows has anywhere to land, the viewer's paying attention, but they don't know why yet.

How To Build Contrast

Once someone's stopped and knows what this is about, there are two ways to build that gap.

1
Say a common belief out loud, then prove it wrong. "Most people think X, they're wrong." You control the whole idea, so it's the safer choice.
2
Skip saying the belief out loud, you just say something strange enough that people have to stop and figure out what you mean themselves. Riskier, but it can land even harder.

Either way, whatever gets promised has to actually show up later in the video. Open the loop, then close it.

Going Deeper
Time To Value

Zero warm-up. No "hey guys," no "so today I want to talk about." The first word is the hook. Every millisecond matters.

The Apple Framework

A key takeaway from Steve Jobs' Apple presentations: lead with a big hook or idea, an unexpected claim, then break that into smaller pieces, why it matters and what it means, and build contrast or small stories on top of each of those pieces, "and it's not because of what you think." Big idea first, smaller pieces second, contrast built throughout, the exact case Apple makes every time.

Section 03

Storyboard & Shot List Notes

The Big Idea

The storyboard is the storytelling layer. It's what gives a video its character and its emotion. It answers what this video is actually about, and how it's supposed to feel. It tells the story and builds the narrative.

The shot list takes that storyboard and adds the creative camera angles on top of it. Different angles introduce different emotions on their own, a low angle says something different than a wide shot, and choosing them on purpose is what makes the narrative land easier and makes the whole piece unmistakably yours. Same storyboard, same story, but the specific angles chosen are what stop someone else from being able to copy it.

Going Deeper

The main angles I typically use are 1:1 and 3/4ths. Beyond that, over-the-shoulder, wide-angle, and POV shots are always fair game too, since they're commonly used and easy to record.

Take away all the fancy words and this is simply a way to map out each individual video before filming it: what it's about, and exactly how each shot gets it there.

Section 05

B-Roll Notes

The Big Idea

Cinematic B-roll isn't random filler. It's very intentional footage that fits the Pillar type, and it's used to show a specific emotion or realization throughout the video.

The Rules
The Main Buckets

Working in the office, during the day or at night, including the miscellaneous office B-roll. Reading and studying. In the gym. Lifestyle content.

The Detail & Cinematic Inserts

Small takeaways or vlog-type pieces. Checking your phone. Texting. On a call. On Zoom. Opening your payment processor. Anything in that same category.

Going Deeper

These are intentional add-ins to the video, used to push the story forward, not just fill space. The viewer never notices it on purpose, it's working underneath the actual footage, covering a cut, backing up whatever emotion is being talked about in that moment, or moving the story to its next beat without anyone realizing it's even there. Talking about grind and discipline, gym footage backs it up. Talking about reflection, reading and studying footage backs it up. The B-roll should always match whatever's actually being said.

Build the vault. Set aside time to shoot footage with no script and no concept attached, just bank it. Nothing recorded is ever wasted, it either gets used now or it gets used later. This is what I call the Content Ecosystem: every clip feeds the next piece of content, nothing disappears the moment it's posted, it just compounds.
